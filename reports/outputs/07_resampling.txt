Coverage check: which interval actually has 95% coverage? (lognormal, n=30)
==============================================================================
  mean:t             coverage=0.843
  mean:percentile    coverage=0.840
  mean:BCa           coverage=0.875
  median:percentile  coverage=0.930
  median:BCa         coverage=0.935
  > For the mean of a heavily skewed variable at n=30, every interval under-covers; BCa is
    the least bad. The t-interval is not terrible either (the CLT is forgiving). The
    bootstrap is not magic at small n with heavy tails: it only sees the tail the sample
    happened to catch.

  SE AR(1) n_eff adjusted    0.00094
  > The iid bootstraps simply reproduce the naive OLS SE because resampling destroys the
    autocorrelation that makes the naive SE wrong. Block bootstrap keeps 2-year chunks
    intact and lands in the same place as HAC and the AR(1) adjustment. If the resampling
    scheme does not preserve the dependence structure, the bootstrap inherits the same
    mistake as the formula.

Jackknife: bias and SE of the skewness of annual precipitation totals
==============================================================================
skewness=0.801  jackknife bias=-0.061  jackknife SE=0.364  bootstrap SE=0.304
bias-corrected skewness = 0.862
  > The jackknife is a deterministic, n-evaluation approximation to the bootstrap. Sample
    skewness is biased toward zero at small n; the jackknife quantifies and corrects it.
    Do not jackknife a median or other non-smooth statistic; its SE estimate is
    inconsistent.

Monte Carlo AR(1) null: p=0.0000  (null SD of t = 1.61 vs 1.0 for iid)
  > Simulating from a fitted null model is the most general calibration tool: fit AR(1) to
    the residuals, generate thousands of trend-free series with the same persistence, and
    ask how often the t-statistic is as large as observed. The null SD of t exceeds 1 by
    roughly sqrt(n/n_eff), the same factor as the analytic adjustment in section 05.
