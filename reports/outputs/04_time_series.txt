Decomposition: classical vs STL
==============================================================================
    [fig] figures/04_time_series/stl.png
Seasonal amplitude (peak-to-trough): 1960s 5.90 ppm, 2010s 6.81 ppm
Remainder SD: classical 0.299, STL 0.266
  > STL lets the seasonal component evolve: the CO2 seasonal amplitude has grown ~15%
    since the 1960s (longer growing seasons, more vigorous Northern Hemisphere
    vegetation). Classical decomposition would average that away into the remainder.

Stationarity tests on CO2 and its differences
==============================================================================
CO2 level                          ADF p=1.000  KPSS p=0.010  -> non-stationary
first difference                   ADF p=0.000  KPSS p=0.100  -> stationary
seasonal difference (lag 12)       ADF p=0.006  KPSS p=0.010  -> ambiguous
first + seasonal difference        ADF p=0.000  KPSS p=0.100  -> stationary
STL remainder                      ADF p=0.000  KPSS p=0.100  -> stationary
  > The level is obviously non-stationary. A first difference still has the seasonal cycle
    (ADF sees stationarity in the mean; KPSS is suspicious); the seasonal difference still
    carries the growth trend; both together give white-ish noise. That is the (d=1, D=1)
    of the SARIMA below. KPSS has an awkward p-value range because it is table-based.
    [fig] figures/04_time_series/acf_pacf.png
  > After double differencing, the ACF has a negative spike at lag 1 and at lag 12 and
    little else: the signature of an MA(1) x seasonal MA(1), i.e. the classic 'airline
    model' SARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)12. Let the data suggest the order, then confirm with AIC.

  > A 6-year horizon is long for a monthly model. The positive bias (0.60 ppm) says the
    truth ran above the forecast: CO2 growth accelerated after 2020 (El Nino 2023-24),
    which a model fit through 2019 cannot know. Interval coverage is 1.00: the intervals
    of a twice-integrated model widen quickly with horizon, so they are wide enough here;
    at shorter horizons ARIMA intervals are usually too narrow because they ignore
    parameter and model uncertainty. Always report a trivial baseline: SARIMA roughly
    halves the seasonal-naive error, which is the honest statement of what the model buys
    you.

==============================================================================
Spectral analysis: where is the variance in frequency?
==============================================================================
    [fig] figures/04_time_series/spectra.png
CO2 dominant frequency: 1.002 cycles/yr (period 1.00 yr); strongest above 1.5 cycles/yr: 2.005
Variance explained by the annual line (f in 0.9-1.1): 0.80
  > CO2's spectrum is a line at 1 cycle/yr plus its harmonic at 2 (the seasonal cycle is
    not a pure sinusoid). Global temperature, once detrended, has a 'red' spectrum: power
    rising toward low frequencies, as any AR(1)/integrating system does. A bump in the 3-7
    year band is ENSO. Red noise is why eyeballing 'cycles' in climate series is
    dangerous: a significance test against an AR(1) background spectrum is required.

==============================================================================
Cross-correlation: CO2 growth rate vs global temperature anomaly
==============================================================================
raw correlation (both trending): r=0.71
detrended max cross-correlation r=0.39 at lag 5 months (positive = temperature leads CO2 growth)
    [fig] figures/04_time_series/cross_correlation.png
  > Two trending series always correlate (r=0.71 raw) and it means nothing. Detrended, the
    cross-correlation peaks at lag +5 months with temperature leading CO2 growth: El Nino
    warmth and drought suppress tropical carbon uptake and raise fire emissions. Growth is
    a trailing 12-month difference centred 6 months back, so relative to the window centre
    global temperature and CO2 growth are nearly coincident (-1 months); tropical Pacific
    SST (Nino 3.4) would lead both by a few more months. The 2/sqrt(n) lines are the 95%
    band for white noise; both series are autocorrelated, so the true band is wider.
